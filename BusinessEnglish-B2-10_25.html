UNIT 1 - Compensation & Employment

at a pay rate / on a salary of
/æt ə peɪ reɪt/ /ɒn ə ˈsæləri ɒv/
Used to specify the amount of money someone earns or the salary level being offered.
The position is offered at a pay rate of $50 per hour, or on a salary of $104,000 per year.
to treat someone fairly
/truːt ˈsʌmwʌn ˈfeəli/
To handle someone with justice and equality, giving them the same respect and consideration as others.
A good manager treats all team members fairly regardless of their background or position.
to establish a pay structure
/ɪˈstæblɪʃ ə peɪ ˈstrʌktʃə/
To create or set up a system that determines how salaries and wages are organized and distributed.
The HR department needs to establish a pay structure that reflects experience levels and job responsibilities.
an incentive
/ɪnˈsentɪv/
Something that encourages or motivates someone to work harder or do better.
Performance bonuses are an incentive for employees to exceed their targets.
to rise in a company hierarchy
/raɪz ɪn ə ˈkʌm.pə.ni haɪˈɑː.rə.ki/
To move up to higher positions in a company with more responsibility.
She rose in the company hierarchy from junior analyst to senior manager.
to bring value to the company
/brɪŋ ˈvæl.juː tə ðə ˈkʌm.pə.ni/
To contribute something useful that helps the company succeed and do better.
Every employee should try to bring value to the company through their work.
organisational behaviour
/ˌɔː.ɡən.aɪˈzeɪ.ʃən.əl bɪˈheɪ.vjər/
The way people act and work together in a company or organization.
Understanding organisational behaviour helps managers create better workplaces.
a good atmosphere
/ə ɡʊd ˈæt.məs.fɪər/
A positive and pleasant feeling at work where people feel happy and comfortable.
The company created a good atmosphere by encouraging teamwork and recognizing good work.
to develop a strategy for
/dɪˈvel.əp ə ˈstræt.ə.dʒi fɔː/
To create a plan to achieve a goal or objective.
The team will develop a strategy for improving how employees stay at the company.
to affect the company structure
/əˈfekt ðə ˈkʌm.pə.ni ˈstrʌk.tʃər/
To cause changes in how a company is organized or how different parts work together.
The merger will affect the company structure and change how departments work.
to enhance the image of the company
/ɪnˈhɑːns ðə ˈɪm.ɪdʒ əv ðə ˈkʌm.pə.ni/
To make people have a better opinion of a company; to make it look better.
Using green practices can enhance the image of the company with customers.
to have an open-plan office
/həv ən ˈəʊ.pən plæn ˈɒf.ɪs/
To have a workspace with few walls or divisions where many employees work in one large area.
Our company has an open-plan office so people can talk more easily to each other.
to have flexibility in
/həv ˈflek.sɪ.bɪl.ə.ti ɪn/
To have the ability to change or adapt; to have freedom in how you do something.
The company offers flexibility in working hours so employees can balance work and family.
diverse work
/daɪˈvɜːs wɜːk/
A variety of different types of tasks and activities in a job.
This job offers diverse work including meetings, writing, and project planning.
to do menial tasks
/duː ˈmiː.ni.əl tɑːsks/
To do simple, routine work that does not require special training or skills.
Even managers sometimes do menial tasks to stay connected with the daily work.
to get a promotion
/ɡet ə prəˈməʊ.ʃən/
To move to a higher job position with more responsibility and usually more money.
After three years of hard work, Maria got a promotion to team lead.
to be on sabbatical
/biː ɒn səˈbæt.ɪ.kəl/
To take a long break from work while keeping your job, to rest, study, or travel.
She took a sabbatical for six months to study and travel.
to have work-life balance
/həv wɜːk laɪf ˈbæl.əns/
To divide your time fairly between work and personal life, family, and free time.
Good companies help employees have work-life balance so they don't feel stressed.
to retain employees
/rɪˈteɪn ɪmˈplɔɪ.iːz/
To keep employees working at a company; to prevent staff from leaving.
The company offers flexible hours to retain employees and reduce staff turnover.
high/low turnover
/haɪ / ləʊ ˈtɜː.nəʊ.vər/
The rate at which employees leave a company (high turnover means many employees leave; low turnover means few employees leave).
The company has low turnover because employees are happy and enjoy working there.
inevitable
/ɪnˈev.ɪ.tə.bəl/
Certain to happen; impossible to avoid or prevent.
With increasing automation, job changes in some industries are inevitable.
to perk someone up
/tə pɜːk ˈsʌm.wʌn ʌp/
To make someone feel happier, more energetic, or more interested; to improve someone's mood or energy.
A small pay raise or recognition can perk employees up and boost their motivation.
to nail something down
/teɪl ˈsʌm.θɪŋ daʊn/
To finalize or confirm something; to establish details firmly so that they cannot be changed.
We need to nail down the project budget and timeline before we start the work.
to be accountable for
/biː əˈkaʊnt.ə.bəl fɔː/
To be responsible and answerable for something; to be required to explain and justify your actions.
Managers are accountable for their team's performance and results.
to concentrate on something/someone
/ˈkɒn.sən.treɪt ɒn/
To focus your attention and effort on one particular task, person, or area.
We need to concentrate on meeting the client's needs and expectations.
to be empowered to do something
/biː ɪmˈpaʊəd tə duː ˈsʌm.θɪŋ/
To be given the authority, confidence, and resources to make decisions and take action.
Employees are empowered to make decisions without always asking their manager first.
to entail something
/ɪnˈteɪl ˈsʌm.θɪŋ/
To involve or require something as a necessary part or consequence.
This job entails managing a team of ten people and preparing monthly reports.
to get on top of things
/ɡet ɒn tɒp əv θɪŋz/
To take control of a situation and manage it effectively; to deal with problems successfully.
The new manager quickly got on top of things and improved team productivity.
to take (something) on board
/teɪk ɒn bɔːrd/
To accept and understand an idea or suggestion; to consider something seriously.
The director took the feedback on board and implemented several recommended changes.
to take on a task
/teɪk ɒn ə tɑːsk/
To accept responsibility for doing a particular job or assignment.
She agreed to take on the project management role for the new product launch.
expertise
/ˌek.spəˈtiːz/
Expert knowledge or skill in a particular area; the ability to do something very well because of extensive practice and study.
His expertise in digital marketing has been invaluable to the company's growth.
corporate
/ˈkɔː.pə.rət/
Related to large businesses or companies; belonging to or shared by all members of a company.
The corporate office sets company-wide policies that all branches must follow.

UNIT 2 - Technology & Software Development

installment
/ɪnˈstɔːl.mənt/
A single payment for something that is being paid for in multiple payments over a period of time; a part of a series or sequence.
You can purchase the software in monthly installments rather than paying the full cost upfront.
to carry out
/ˈkær.i aʊt/
To perform or complete a task; to execute a plan or activity.
The team will carry out extensive testing before the software release.
to come up with
/kʌm ʌp wɪð/
To think of or create an idea or solution; to produce or develop something.
We need to come up with a better algorithm to optimize database queries.
to pay off
/peɪ ɒf/
To result in success or benefit; to have a positive outcome after effort or investment.
Learning Python really paid off when I started working in data analysis.
to take up
/teɪk ʌp/
To begin or start doing something; to occupy space or time.
I'm not very good at golf – I only took it up recently. / Cloud storage takes up less physical space than traditional servers.
to bring down
/brɪŋ daʊn/
To cause to fail or stop working; to reduce or lower something.
A DDoS attack can bring down an entire website within minutes. / They've really brought down the price of electric cars.
to bring about
/brɪŋ əˈbaʊt/
To cause something to happen; to create or produce a result or change.
Artificial intelligence has brought about major changes in software development.
to take forward
/teɪk ˈfɔː.wərd/
To continue developing or progressing with something; to move a project or idea to the next stage.
Management decided to take forward the mobile app project to the next phase.
to get round
/ɡet raʊnd/
To find a solution to a problem; to avoid an obstacle or difficulty.
The developers found a way to get round the compatibility issues and the restrictions.
to set up
/set ʌp/
To install, configure, or establish something; to put something in place and prepare it for use.
It took two hours to set up the new server environment.
mastery of
/ˈmɑːs.tər.i ɒv/
Complete skill or knowledge of something, achieved through extensive practice and study.
She demonstrated mastery of multiple programming languages during the technical interview.
to embed
/ɪmˈbed/
To place or fix something firmly in a surrounding mass; to integrate or incorporate something into a system.
The security features were embedded directly into the application code.
to accelerate
/əkˈsel.ə.reɪt/
To increase in speed or rate; to speed up a process or development.
Cloud technology can accelerate the deployment of new features to production.
a connoisseur
/ˌkɒn.əˈsɜː/
A person with extensive knowledge and refined taste in a particular area; an expert or enthusiast.
He is a connoisseur of open-source software and always recommends the best tools for the job.
to take the lead
/teɪk ðə liːd/
To be the first to do something; to assume a position of leadership or control in a situation or project.
She decided to take the lead on the new initiative and drive the team forward.
know like the back of our hands
/nəʊ laɪk ðə bæk əv aʊə hændz/
To know something extremely well; to be very familiar with a place, subject, or skill.
The team members know the software like the back of their hands after working with it for five years.
It's your call on
/ɪts jɔː kɔːl ɒn/
It is your decision or choice about something; you have the authority to make the final decision.
It's your call on whether we proceed with the project or wait for more information.
You might not like what I'm about to say but
/juː maɪt nɒt laɪk wɒt aɪm əˈbaʊt tə seɪ bʌt/
A phrase used to warn someone before you deliver difficult, negative, or critical feedback; to prepare them for unwelcome news.
You might not like what I'm about to say but the project is behind schedule and we need to make some tough decisions.
to pick up on
/pɪk ʌp ɒn/
To notice or become aware of something; to understand something that someone has implied or suggested.
The manager quickly picked up on the tension in the team during the meeting.
makes sense
/meɪks sens/
To be logical, clear, or understandable; to be a reasonable or sensible idea.
Your proposal makes sense from a budget perspective, and the team agrees it's the right direction.

UNIT 3 - Money Issues

to put money aside for something
/tə pʊt ˈmʌn.i əˈsaɪd fɔː ˈsʌm.θɪŋ/
To save money for a specific purpose or future need; to reserve funds.
We need to put money aside for our children's education.
to shell out (a lot of money) for something
/tə ʃel aʊt (ə lɒt əv ˈmʌn.i) fɔː ˈsʌm.θɪŋ/
To spend a large amount of money, often reluctantly; to pay out money.
We had to shell out a lot of money for the car repairs.
to splash out on something
/tə splæʃ aʊt ɒn ˈsʌm.θɪŋ/
To spend a large amount of money on something enjoyable or luxurious; to treat yourself.
They decided to splash out on a luxury vacation after working hard all year.
to cut back on something
/tə kʌt bæk ɒn ˈsʌm.θɪŋ/
To reduce spending or use of something; to use less of something to save money.
The company decided to cut back on unnecessary expenses to improve profitability.
to take out a mortgage
/tə teɪk aʊt ə ˈmɔː.ɡɪdʒ/
To borrow a large amount of money from a bank to purchase property, which is repaid over many years.
They took out a mortgage to buy their first home.
to pay off a loan
/tə peɪ ɒf ə ləʊn/
To repay all the money borrowed; to complete payment of a debt.
It took them fifteen years to pay off the loan completely.
to build a nest egg
/tə bɪld ə nest eɡ/
To save money gradually over time for future security or retirement; to accumulate savings.
By investing regularly, she's been building a nest egg for her retirement.
to invest in something
/tə ɪnˈvest ɪn ˈsʌm.θɪŋ/
To put money into something with the hope of making a profit or gaining a return; to commit funds.
The company decided to invest in new technology to improve productivity.
to boost something
/tə buːst ˈsʌm.θɪŋ/
To increase, improve, or strengthen something; to give a boost to.
The marketing campaign helped boost sales by 30% this quarter.
the bottom line
/ðə ˈbɒt.əm laɪn/
The most important fact or result; the final profit or loss in financial statements.
The bottom line is that we need to reduce costs to stay competitive.
to make up ground
/tə meɪk ʌp ɡraʊnd/
To recover lost time or progress; to catch up; to compensate for delays or losses.
The sales team worked hard to make up ground after a slow start to the year.
to make a mark on something
/tə meɪk ə mɑːk ɒn ˈsʌm.θɪŋ/
To have a significant impact or effect on something; to leave an impression.
Her innovative ideas have made a mark on the industry and changed how companies operate.
to rectify
/ˈrek.tɪ.faɪ/
To correct or fix something that is wrong; to put right a mistake or error.
The company took immediate action to rectify the billing error that affected customers.
to give someone a prior notice
/tə ɡɪv ˈsʌm.wʌn ə ˈpraɪ.ər ˈnəʊ.tɪs/
To inform someone in advance about something that will happen; to provide advance warning.
The company gave employees prior notice of the office relocation to allow them time to prepare.
go bankrupt
/ɡəʊ ˈbæŋk.rʌpt/
To lose all your money and be unable to pay debts; to fail financially and be declared legally insolvent.
The company went bankrupt after losing major contracts and was forced to close.
bail out
/beɪl aʊt/
To rescue a company or person from financial difficulty by providing money; to help someone out of trouble.
The government decided to bail out the bank to prevent a financial crisis.
to boom
/tə buːm/
To experience rapid growth and success; to become very popular or prosperous.
The technology sector boomed during the 1990s and created many new jobs.
to drop prices
/tə drɒp ˈpraɪ.sɪz/
To reduce the cost of something; to lower prices in order to sell more or compete.
The retailer decided to drop prices on winter clothing to clear inventory.
to recover from the recession
/tə rɪˈkʌv.ər frɒm ðə rɪˈseʃ.ən/
To return to economic growth and stability after a period of economic decline and reduced business activity.
The economy is slowly recovering from the recession as unemployment decreases.
to lend money to someone
/tə lend ˈmʌn.i tə ˈsʌm.wʌn/
To give money to someone with the expectation that they will return it in the future, often with interest.
The bank will lend money to someone with a good credit score and stable income.
to borrow money from someone
/tə ˈbɒr.əʊ ˈmʌn.i frɒm ˈsʌm.wʌn/
To take money from someone with the intention of returning it in the future, often with interest.
The small business decided to borrow money from the bank to expand its operations.
