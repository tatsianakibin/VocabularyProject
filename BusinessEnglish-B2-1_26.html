UNIT 1 - First Impressions

ineffective (negative)
/ˌɪn.ɪˈfek.tɪv/
Not producing the desired result or effect; failing to achieve the intended purpose.
An ineffective method can waste time and resources. The company's ineffective strategy led to a loss of market share.
arrogant (negative)
/ˈær.ə.ɡənt/
Having an exaggerated sense of one's own importance or abilities; displaying excessive pride or self-importance.
His arrogant attitude made it difficult to work with him. The arrogant behavior of the CEO alienated the team.
functional (neutral/positive)
/ˈfʌŋk.ʃən.əl/
Designed to be practical and useful rather than attractive; serving a purpose effectively.
The functional design of the office maximizes productivity. A functional approach to problem-solving is often more effective than a creative one.
modest (positive)
/ˈmɒd.ɪst/
Not large or expensive; humble and moderate in size, scope, or ambition.
She works from a modest office. The company made a modest proposal for the merger. He drives a modest phone and uses a modest car.
ostentatious (negative)
/ˌɒs.tenˈteɪ.ʃəs/
Designed to impress or attract notice; excessively showy and meant to display wealth or importance.
The ostentatious display of wealth made others uncomfortable. His ostentatious lifestyle and over-the-top car reflected his insecurity.
trustworthy (positive)
/ˈtrʌst.wɜː.ðɪ/
Able to be relied on as honest or truthful; deserving of confidence and trust.
A trustworthy source is essential for accurate research. She is a trustworthy person who always delivers on her commitments.
suspicious (negative)
/səˈspɪʃ.əs/
Having or showing a feeling that something is wrong or someone is behaving badly; distrustful.
Suspicious behavior can undermine trust in the workplace. The circumstances surrounding the deal were highly suspicious. A suspicious-looking van was parked outside. I am suspicious about his motives.
complex (neutral)
/ˈkɒm.pleks/
Consisting of many different and connected parts; complicated and difficult to understand or deal with.
Solving a complex problem requires careful analysis. A complex system with multiple variables needs thorough testing before implementation.
wary (neutral/negative)
/ˈweə.ri/
Cautious and careful; alert to possible danger or problems; showing caution or suspicion.
Be wary of strangers offering unsolicited advice. Employees remain wary of the new management system. Many parents are wary about standardized tests.
favourable (positive)
/ˈfeɪ.vər.ə.bəl/
Expressing approval or agreement; advantageous and likely to lead to success or a positive outcome.
The interview made a favourable impression on the hiring committee. Favourable conditions in the market made it an ideal time to launch the product.
monotonous (negative)
/məˈnɒt.ən.əs/
Lacking variety or interest; tedious and boring because of being unchanging or repetitive.
The monotonous tone of voice made the presentation difficult to follow. Working on monotonous tasks all day can lead to decreased productivity and employee dissatisfaction.

UNIT 2 - Phrasal Verbs

carry (carried-carried) out
/ˈkær.i aʊt/
To perform or complete a task; to execute a plan or activity.
The team will carry out extensive testing before the software release. We carried out a survey to understand customer needs.
come up with
/kʌm ʌp wɪð/
To think of or create an idea or solution; to produce or develop something.
We need to come up with a better algorithm to optimize database queries. The team came up with an innovative approach to solve the problem.
pay off
/peɪ ɒf/
To result in success or benefit; to have a positive outcome after effort or investment.
Learning Python really paid off when I started working in data analysis. The investment in new technology will eventually pay off.
take up
/teɪk ʌp/
To begin or start doing something; to occupy space or time.
I'm not very good at golf – I only took it up recently. I took up painting and walking around my district. Cloud storage takes up less physical space than traditional servers. Our class takes up an hour and 20 minutes.
bring (brought-brought) down
/brɪŋ daʊn/
To cause to fail or stop working; to reduce or lower something.
A DDoS attack can bring down an entire website within minutes. They've really brought down the price of electric cars. The system outage brought down our online services.
bring about
/brɪŋ əˈbaʊt/
To cause something to happen; to create or produce a result or change.
Artificial intelligence has brought about major changes in software development. The new policy brought about significant improvements in workplace culture.
take forward
/teɪk ˈfɔː.wərd/
To continue developing or progressing with something; to move a project or idea to the next stage.
Management decided to take forward the mobile app project to the next phase. We will take forward the proposal at next month's meeting.
get round
/ɡet raʊnd/
To find a solution to a problem; to avoid an obstacle or difficulty.
The developers found a way to get round the compatibility issues. They managed to get round the bug, the restrictions, and the problems that arose.
set up
/set ʌp/
To install, configure, or establish something; to put something in place and prepare it for use.
It took two hours to set up the new server environment. We need to set up a new workstation for the incoming employee.

UNIT 3 - On Schedule

upfront planning
/ˈʌpfrʌnt ˈplænɪŋ/
Planning done at the beginning, before starting work; preparation and organization completed before a project begins.
Good upfront planning helps avoid problems later in the project. Do upfront planning to ensure the project runs smoothly.
budget constraints
/ˈbʌdʒɪt kənˈstreɪnts/
Limitations on the money available to spend; restrictions on financial resources.
We had to simplify the design due to budget constraints. Work within budget constraints to ensure the project remains financially viable.
accurate forecast
/ˈækjərət ˈfɔːrkæst/
A prediction that is correct or very close to reality; an estimate of future costs, timeline, or resources that is precise.
An accurate forecast helps managers allocate resources properly. Make an accurate forecast before starting the project.
realistic budget
/ˌriːəˈlɪstɪk ˈbʌdʒɪt/
A financial plan based on what is actually possible; a budget that accurately reflects real costs and circumstances.
We need to create a realistic budget that accounts for unexpected costs and market changes.
miss the deadline
/mɪs ðə ˈded.laɪn/
Fail to complete something by the required time; not finish work by the agreed-upon date.
If we miss the deadline, we'll lose the client and damage our reputation.
run into problems
/rʌn ˈɪn.tə ˈprɒb.ləmz/
Face unexpected difficulties; encounter issues or obstacles during a project.
We ran into problems when the supplier delayed the shipment. The team will run into problems if resources are not allocated properly.
stay on track
/steɪ ɒn træk/
Continue making progress according to plan; maintain the planned schedule and timeline.
Daily meetings help the team stay on track with their goals. We need to stay on track to meet the project deadline.
go over the budget
/ɡəʊ ˈəʊ.vər ðə ˈbʌdʒɪt/
Spend more money than planned; exceed the allocated financial resources.
The construction project went over the budget by 20%. If we go over the budget, we'll need to cut expenses elsewhere.
fall behind schedule
/fɔːl bɪˈhaɪnd ˈʃedjuːl/
Become late compared to the planned timeline; progress slower than expected.
Bad weather caused us to fall behind schedule. We cannot fall behind schedule or we'll miss the launch date.
make the launch date
/meɪk ðə ˈlɔːntʃ deɪt/
Successfully complete work by the planned release time; meet the scheduled deadline for launching a product or project.
The team worked overtime to make the launch date. We must make the launch date to stay competitive in the market.
to come along (=develop)
/kʌm əˈlɒŋ/
To progress or develop; to make progress over time; to advance toward completion.
The project is coming along nicely and should be ready for launch next month. How is your work coming along? Things are coming along well despite some initial challenges.
to be on track
/biː ɒn træk/
To be progressing according to plan; to be on schedule and making expected progress toward a goal.
The project is on track to meet the deadline. We are on track to achieve our quarterly targets. The team believes the launch is on track.
to hit a problem
/hɪt ə ˈprɒb.ləm/
To encounter or face an unexpected difficulty or obstacle; to suddenly encounter a problem.
We hit a problem when the supplier failed to deliver on time. The team hit some problems during testing. If we hit any problems, we'll address them immediately.
to be about to
/biː əˈbaʊt tə/
To be on the verge of doing something; to be very close to the moment when something will happen.
The company is about to launch a new product line. We are about to announce the merger. The deadline is about to arrive.

UNIT 5 - Ethics & Values

bribery and corruption
/ˈbraɪ.bər.i/ and /kəˈrʌp.ʃən/
Illegal payment or money given or received to influence business decisions; dishonest behaviour by officials.
The company was investigated for bribery and corruption in international contracts. Many developing nations struggle with corruption in their government procurement processes.
to face an ethical dilemma
/tuː/ /feɪs/ /ən/ /ˌɛθ.ɪ.kəl/ /dɪˈlem.ə/
Encounter a difficult situation where moral or ethical principles are in conflict with business objectives.
The manager faced an ethical dilemma when asked to hide safety violations from regulators. Many leaders face ethical dilemmas between maximizing profits and protecting the environment.
to involve (involved-involved) deception
/tuː/ /ɪnˈvɒlv/ /dɪˈsep.ʃən/
Include or contain dishonest or misleading information; to deceive or mislead someone deliberately.
The fraudulent scheme involved deception of investors about company finances. Marketing practices that involve deception are illegal in most countries.
to take (took-taken) responsibility for
/tuː/ /teɪk/ /rɪˌspɒn.sɪˈbɪl.ɪ.ti/ /fɔːr/
Accept accountability or duty for something; acknowledge that you are answerable for actions or outcomes.
The CEO took responsibility for the product defect and launched a comprehensive recall. Good leaders take responsibility for mistakes rather than blaming their team.
to experience fair treatment
/tuː/ /ɪkˈspɪə.rɪ.əns/ /feər/ /ˈtriːt.mənt/
Receive equal and just treatment; be treated without discrimination or favouritism.
All employees should experience fair treatment regardless of gender, age, or background. Workers in many industries do not experience fair treatment in wages and working conditions.
an act of generosity
/ən/ /ækt/ /əv/ /dʒenˈɒrəsəti/
A kind or charitable action showing willingness to give time, money, or help without expecting return.
The company's donation to the charity was an act of generosity that improved their brand reputation. His act of generosity in mentoring junior employees demonstrated his commitment to their development.
core values
/kɔːr/ /ˈvæl.juːz/
Fundamental beliefs and principles that guide an organisation's or individual's behaviour and decision-making.
Our company's core values include integrity, innovation, and customer focus. Employees who don't align with the organisation's core values struggle to fit in.
to have prejudice against someone
/tuː/ /hæv/ /ˈpredʒ.ə.dɪs/ /əˈɡenst/
Hold an unfair or negative opinion about someone based on their group, characteristics, or background rather than facts.
Some hiring managers have prejudice against candidates from certain educational backgrounds. Unconscious bias means people often have prejudice against groups without realising it.
to lose credibility
/tuː/ /luːz/ /krəˈdɪb.ɪl.ɪ.ti/
Lose the quality of being trusted or believed; lose respect due to dishonest or unreliable behaviour.
The politician lost credibility after making false promises to voters. A company loses credibility if it fails to deliver on its product guarantees.
to be driven by greed
/tuː/ /bi/ /ˈdrɪv.ən/ /baɪ/ /ɡriːd/
Be motivated by excessive desire for money, wealth, or material gain at the expense of ethics or others.
Many financial scandals occur because executives are driven by greed rather than business principles. Companies driven by greed often lose customer loyalty and face legal consequences.
to face discrimination
/tuː/ /feɪs/ /dɪˌskrɪm.ɪˈneɪ.ʃən/
Experience unfair or unequal treatment based on protected characteristics such as race, gender, age, or disability.
Workers face discrimination in salary negotiations based on their gender despite having equal qualifications. People over 55 often face discrimination in hiring practices despite their experience and skills.
